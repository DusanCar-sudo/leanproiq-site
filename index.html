What this work actually is.

An AI model writes an answer. Somebody has to decide whether that answer was good — and explain why, in writing, clearly enough that an engineer can use the explanation to improve the model.

That somebody is you. The industry calls it data annotation, AI evaluation, RLHF, or model alignment. They mostly mean the same thing: reading carefully and judging honestly, in writing.

It is not programming. It is not machine learning. Most of it is closer to marking essays than to writing software.

A real task, roughly
User asks: "My landlord hasn't returned my deposit. It's been two months since I moved out. What can I do?"
Model answer A

You should contact your landlord and request the deposit. If they refuse, you may wish to seek legal advice. Many jurisdictions have tenant protection laws that may apply to your situation.

Model answer B

Most jurisdictions require deposits to be returned within 30 days. Send a written demand by recorded delivery stating the date you vacated and the amount owed. If unpaid after 14 days, small claims court is usually the next step — in many places you can claim up to three times the deposit.

Your job: which is better, and why — in two or three sentences an engineer can act on.
What a good answer looks like

B is better on actionability. A tells the user to "seek legal advice" without naming a single step, which leaves them exactly where they started. B gives a sequence they can follow tomorrow. However, B states specific timeframes and a damages multiplier without noting these vary by jurisdiction — a user could act on the wrong number. B wins, but should be flagged for unqualified legal specifics.

That is the job. Notice what it required: careful reading, a named criterion, a specific flaw, and clear writing. No code, no maths, no AI background.

Some projects are more specialised — evaluating code, medical text, or a particular language. Those pay more. But the shape of the work stays the same.

Before you invest a weekend in this

Whether you'd actually be any good at it.

This suits some people enormously and makes others miserable. It is worth knowing which you are before you spend two weeks applying.

This tends to suit you if
  • You write clearly in English and can explain why something is wrong, not just that it is
  • You have real professional depth in something — nursing, law, accounting, teaching, logistics, a trade, a language
  • You are comfortable working alone with no manager and no colleagues
  • You can follow a long, fussy instruction document exactly
  • You want irregular hours that fit around something else
This tends not to suit you if
  • You need steady, predictable income this month — work arrives in bursts and droughts
  • You want to be told what to do and get feedback as you go. Mostly you get neither
  • Repetitive close reading drains you. Some batches are genuinely dull
  • You want a job title, a team, a contract, sick pay. This is freelance, project by project
  • You are hoping for passive income. Every dollar here is an hour worked
The honest version

Most people who try this stop within a month. Not because it was too hard — because they were rejected by two platforms, waited three weeks for a task that never came, and concluded it was a scam. It usually isn't a scam. It is just slow, administrative, and nobody warns you about the queue.

Four things the landing pages leave out

Rates are adjusted by country. Several platforms scale pay to local cost of living. The same task pays differently in Berlin and in Lagos.

Read the unit, not the number. Some audio projects pay per finished hour of audio, not per hour you work. Contributors report an advertised $35–45 collapsing to about $9 an hour actually worked.

The gap between accepted and paid is the real cost. Onboarding takes an afternoon. Waiting for a first project can take weeks. One documented account finished every onboarding step, was invited to a $60–120/hr role, and had earned $0.00.

Some evaluation phases are unpaid. A minority of projects gate payment on reviewer acceptance after you have already done hours of real work. Read the payment terms in the project description before you start. Every time.

A realistic first three months

Two to four weeks applying and waiting. A first small batch of work. A first payment somewhere between $50 and $300. If it goes well, a few hundred dollars a month at ten to fifteen hours a week, rising as your quality history builds.

It is a second income that can grow into a first one. It is not a rescue from a bad month. If you need money in ten days, this is the wrong door — and I would rather tell you that now than sell you a course.

What actually stops people

Four walls. None of them are about skill.

Almost everyone who fails at this fails before they ever see a task. Not because the work was too hard — because of an administrative wall nobody warned them about.

WALL 01

Citizenship vs. residence

If your profile says one country and your ID says another, verification fails — and most platforms give you two or three attempts total. Which country you declare is a decision to make deliberately, before you sign up, not a form field to fill in casually.

WALL 02

Silent geography gates

Several well-known platforms simply don't operate where you live, and won't say so until after you've built a profile. Others accept your signup but show no available tasks. Knowing which is which saves weeks.

WALL 03

Getting paid, actually

PayPal, Payoneer, Stripe and local banks each fail differently depending on where you are. Name spelling mismatches, foreign-currency restrictions, annual fees on low volume, and biometric thresholds can all hold your first payment.

WALL 04

Writing like an evaluator

Most applicants are screened out on a written sample. The bar isn't fluency — it's whether your rationale is specific, falsifiable, and useful to someone training a model. That's a learnable skill and almost nobody teaches it.

On price

Fair pricing, said out loud.

Plenty of companies already do this. They read your location, quietly adjust the number, and never mention it. I'd rather write it down and let you check the arithmetic.

The same hour of my time costs a different amount of life depending on where you're standing. Sixty dollars is an evening out in Berlin and most of a week's food in Lagos. Charging both people sixty dollars isn't neutral — it just hides the unfairness somewhere you can't see it.

So the price scales with your country's purchasing power, using published GDP-per-capita PPP figures. Same session, same preparation, same attention. Different number, for a stated reason.

One price for everyone is not the same thing as treating everyone equally.

I benefit from this too

I'm a Serbian citizen living in Da Nang. My own costs are low, which is precisely why I can price an hour in Nigeria at thirty dollars without it being charity. This isn't generosity — it's arithmetic that happens to work.

The floor is for me

I can earn around twenty-eight dollars an hour doing evaluation work directly. Below that, teaching costs me money rather than earning it. The floor isn't a sales tactic. It's the line where this stops being something I can keep doing.

No proof required

I won't ask for a utility bill, check your IP address, or make you prove anything. Tell me where you are and I'll take your word for it. If the adjusted number is still out of reach, write and say so — that conversation is not a problem.

What it costs

Priced against your economy, not mine.

Charging one flat rate worldwide would mean someone in Lagos pays four times as much of their income as someone in Berlin for the same hour. So prices scale with GDP per capita at purchasing power parity, floored so the work stays sustainable for me.
